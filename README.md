# Compile Now, Wait, or Build Ahead? torch.compile and CUDA Graphs for few-step diffusion serving

Code, raw result tables, and the paper source for the study *Compile Now, Wait, or Build Ahead? Amortizing
torch.compile and CUDA Graphs Across Request Shapes in Few-Step Diffusion Serving* (authors: Kunlin Yang and Ibrahim (Abe) M. Elfadel;
paper in `paper/`: `main.pdf` is the submission version, `main_long.pdf` the longer draft).

## One-sentence result

On one RTX 4090 with Stable Diffusion 1.5 + LCM-LoRA (fp16, 4 steps), an online, reuse-aware, memory-budgeted
shape-cache policy does **not** beat compiling every shape on first sight (the pre-registered hypothesis is
refuted: 4.4% slower in simulation at 10^4 requests, and even an oracle compile-or-eager selection could gain
under 1%); the one clear real speed-up comes from moving the one-time cost offline: ahead-of-time (AOT)
packages need 0.890 of eager's total service time at 10^3 requests per process and 0.845 at 10^4 (offline build
excluded from service time), and this gain only pays when the build is shared by many processes. All numbers in
the paper are generated by the scripts here from the result tables in `results/` (see "Paper numbers" below).

## Why the primary hypothesis failed (and why no online policy can meet it here)

1. **What was tested.** Pre-registered hypothesis H4a: on a skewed multi-shape stream (W2), 10^4 requests per process, warm
   disk compile cache, our online reuse-aware policy is at least 2% faster than compiling every shape on first sight (B1),
   on at least 8 of 10 held-out seeds. Result: 4.4% *slower* on 10/10 seeds (CI [4.3, 4.5]%); a real 10^4 replay
   reproduced the ordering (policy 4.5% behind B1).
2. **Why it failed.** From a warm disk cache a new shape compiles in about 4.4 s and then runs 16.5% faster, so almost every
   shape repays within a few hundred requests (break-even n* = 304 at 512x512). Waiting to see reuse before compiling
   therefore pays roughly one extra one-time cost per shape.
3. **The ceiling.** B1's whole one-time cost is only 7.34% of its total time at 10^4 requests (hard ceiling for anything that
   keeps B1's steady state). An oracle that knows every shape's future count and picks compile-or-eager per shape gains only
   0.83% over B1. The 2% line was out of reach for any online compile-or-eager rule.
4. **What else we tried, and why each failed (all with their own pre-registered lines):** a revised admission rule (v3,
   1.047x B1: any rule blind to N loses early at 10^3 or late at 10^4); compiling in a background thread (eager requests took
   about 9.6x their normal time due to interpreter-lock contention; no net gain); a subprocess pre-warm (still blocks about
   5.8 s while loading); the official Inductor tuning recipe (3-5% faster steady state, but 1.088x B1 as a service because it
   re-tunes in every process).
5. **Why it is out of reach in principle.** Compile-or-eager is the ski-rental problem: a rule that does not know the future
   count pays about one extra one-time cost whenever compiling at once was right, and with warm caches compiling at once is
   almost always right. Gains beyond the ceiling need a faster steady state (the graph layer adds only 1.7%) or removing the
   one-time cost from the request path, which is what the offline AOT route does (0.890 of eager at 10^3, 0.845 at 10^4;
   0.760 on an H200). The bound is specific to warm caches and long-lived processes: from empty caches the policy avoids
   B1's 4.44x loss (it ties eager), which is an avoided loss, not a speed-up.

## Hardware and software

See `docs/hardware_software.md`. Main platform: Intel Core i9-14900KF, 62 GB RAM, one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090
(24 GB, driver 535.309.01), Ubuntu 24.04, Python 3.11, torch 2.9.1+cu126, diffusers 0.35.2. CPU-only parts
(the main figure, tables, simulator, tests) run on any Linux machine with Python 3.11 and ~8 GB of RAM; only
`--quick` and the real-GPU experiments need a GPU. Timing claims are only valid on the platform they were
measured on; the H200 replay in the paper is reported separately and never mixed with 4090 numbers.

## Reproduce the main figure (three layers)

All commands run from the repository root with `PYTHONPATH=$PWD/code`.

| layer | command | needs | expected time |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) redraw from the raw table | `python -m p24.reproduce_main_figure --from-csv results/S3/sim_eval/main_raw.csv --out out_a` | CPU | seconds |
| (b) re-run the request-level simulator | `python -m p24.reproduce_main_figure --out out_b --procs 4` | CPU, frozen cost table, evaluation traces (below) | about 3 to 11 min (4 to 8 processes) |
| (c) real-GPU scaled-down check | `python -m p24.reproduce_main_figure --quick` | one 24 GB GPU, model weights (below) | <= 15 min target, from an empty compile cache |

* Layer (b) writes `out_b/main_raw.csv` and `out_b/compare_vs_frozen.json`: every column except wall-clock
  columns must equal the frozen `results/S3/sim_eval/main_raw.csv` (`n_mismatch_cells = 0`). PNG bytes are only
  identical in the original environment (the reference SHA-256 prefix of `fig_main_W2.png` is `e6d8122a695c569a`).
* Evaluation traces: the 128 traces with N <= 1000 ship with the repository (`results/S3/workload`).
  The 125 larger ones (N = 10^4, 10^5) are rebuilt with `python -m scripts.rebuild_traces` after placing the DiffusionDB
  metadata at `data/diffusiondb/metadata.parquet` (see `docs/DATASET.md`); the script uses the frozen generator and checks
  every trace against `results/S3/workload/sha256_manifest.txt` (about 1 min). The simulator never reads prompt text.
* `--quick` loads the fused SD1.5 + LCM-LoRA model; see "Models" below. It compares the prediction of the frozen
  cost-table model with a measured scaled-down trace; the pass criteria (for example predicted vs measured total service
  time within 15%, GPU memory hard cap 14 GB) are listed at the top of `code/scripts/reproduce_quick_S4.py`.
  It needs the fused model at `data/models/sd15_lcm_fused/` and writes temporary compile caches under `tmp/`
  (deleted afterwards). Other GPU scripts keep compile caches under `cache/p24/` unless `P24_CACHE` is set.
  Note: the recorded run (2026-11-24) used `--out` with an absolute path; a relative `--out` failed in an earlier attempt
  because the serving sub-processes run in another working directory. The script now resolves `--out` to an absolute
  path (one line, `code/scripts/reproduce_quick_S4.py`, after the recorded run; criteria and computation unchanged).

above: CPU tests 0 failures; layer (a) PNG SHA-256 prefix `e6d8122a695c569a`; paper numbers built twice with identical
outputs; 125 traces rebuilt, 253/253 match the manifest; layer (b) 7800/7800 rows, `n_mismatch_cells = 0`
(`results/S5/fresh_env/compare_vs_frozen_v3.json`); layer (c) `--quick` on the RTX 4090 from an empty compile cache: all
pre-registered criteria pass, wall time 259 s, steady-state latency within 1.6% of the frozen cost table, predicted vs measured

ordering of eager and our policy on this 60-request trace was reversed relative to the prediction (6.600 s vs 6.621 s, 0.3%);

## Paper numbers, tables and figures

`python -m p24.paper.build_all` regenerates the registry CSVs, the tables, the number macros (`paper/paper/numbers.tex`)
and the main figures from the result tables, and prints one SHA per output. Every number in the paper text is a macro
traced to a result file (`paper/paper/numbers_manifest.csv`: macro, value, source file, selector, generating script).
Further scripts: `code/scripts/regime_fig_v2_S5.py` (regime map), `code/scripts/ceiling_S4X.py` (kernel breakdown and ceiling),
`code/scripts/analyze_rare_evict_S5.py` (rare shapes and evictions). Build the paper with
`cd paper/latex && tectonic main_v7.tex` (or any LaTeX with IEEEtran).

## Profiling traces

`results/S5/profiling_traces/` holds kernel-level traces exported from the Nsight Systems captures used in the paper
(19 traces: 512x512 eager/compiled/captured variants, 256x256 and 768x768 capture checks, the SD-Turbo check). Each
`*.kernels.csv` lists every GPU kernel and memcpy inside the three steady-state requests (short kernel name, start, duration,
stream, grid/block, CUDA-graph node); `index.csv` gives the SHA-256 of the source capture. The original `.nsys-rep`/`.sqlite`
files embed host metadata and are not distributed; `code/scripts/export_kernel_traces_S5.py` regenerates the CSVs from them.

## Provenance of hashes

Pre-registration cards, verdict files and logs record SHA-256 hashes of the *internal* (untranslated) files. For every
exported file, `MANIFEST.sha256` gives the hash of the exported bytes and `docs/export_manifest.csv` gives both the
exported hash and the hash of the internal source file (`src_sha256`), so a hash quoted in a card can be matched to its exported counterpart. Translated `.py` files differ from
their internal sources only in comments, docstrings and message strings (checked by an AST comparison).
Four files are checked by hash at run time by the frozen simulator guard and are therefore shipped byte-identical, with
their original (partly Chinese) comments: `code/p24/policy/base.py`, `code/p24/policy/ours.py`, `code/p24/sim/prior.py` and
`results/S3/workload/sha256_manifest.txt`; English copies of the three `.py` files are in `docs/frozen_code_en/`. The frozen
`prior.py` reads its statistics file under the internal directory name, so the repository contains one compatibility link
(a top-level directory with the internal, Chinese name of `results/`, holding only a link to
`results/S2/workload/workload_stats.json`).
A few `% src:` comments in the paper pointed to internal working reports written in Chinese; those reports are not part
of this repository and are listed in `docs/internal_sources.md` (with hashes). Every number they support is also
reproducible from the result tables named in the same comments or in `paper/paper/numbers_manifest.csv`.
Comments and messages in the scripts sometimes mention "the main session": this is the coordinating researcher of the
study, who scheduled GPU blocks and approved pre-registration cards.

## Limitations

Declared (not measured) batch and guidance distributions; one sequential serving process without arrival times,
queueing or tail latency; one GPU model (RTX 4090) as the main platform (cost model and AOT also checked on an H200);
serving results for one model (mechanism also checked on SD-Turbo); the offline AOT build time is excluded from service
time and its amortization is simulated; bit-identity under a binding memory cap is not shown; GPU clocks were not locked.
See Sec. VIII and the appendix of the paper for the full list.
